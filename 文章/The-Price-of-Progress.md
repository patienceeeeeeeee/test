# The Price of Progress

> You can shorten the journey, but you cannot skip the fundamentals.

*An English reflection by Miles Huang · October 18, 2026*

Sometimes, I wish success could come overnight.

I want to speak English fluently, understand the work I do, and become someone who can solve difficult problems. It is tempting to believe that a few intense days could give me the ability I admire in others.

But a quick result is not the same as real ability.

## The Work Behind What Looks Easy

When someone gives a confident presentation in English, I see the finished performance. I do not see the awkward conversations, misunderstood questions, or countless small corrections that may have come before it.

I could memorize a fifteen-minute speech in a few days. Yet if someone asked me an unexpected question, I might struggle to answer. The script would be ready, but my ability to think and respond in English would still need practice.

The same distinction matters at work. A new engineer can learn technical terms quickly. Knowing those words, however, does not mean knowing how to judge a manufacturing problem, check the evidence, and decide when to ask for help.

**Knowing the words is not the same as knowing what to do.**

## There Is a Cost, but Not a Fixed Price

I used to think that every achievement required a fixed amount of effort. Now I think this idea needs one important correction.

Every meaningful achievement has requirements, but people do not always pay the same price to meet them. A good teacher, an effective tool, useful feedback, or earlier experience can make the journey much shorter.

Working harder in the wrong direction does not necessarily bring me closer to my goal. Sometimes the most valuable step is to stop repeating a mistake and change my method.

**You can shorten the journey, but you cannot skip the fundamentals.**

## What Business Teaches Me About Progress

Starting a small business may take only a short time. Building one that earns a sustainable profit is a different challenge.

Imagine selling a desk fan. Receiving the first order feels exciting, but that order alone tells me little about whether the business will survive. I still need to understand product cost, shipping, advertising, returns, and whether customers will buy again.

Selling more units does not automatically mean earning more money. If the cost of acquiring customers is greater than the money left from each sale, growth can make the losses bigger.

A busy business is not necessarily a healthy business. And a busy person is not necessarily making progress.

## My Standard for Tomorrow

I do not want to use the phrase 'success takes time' as an excuse for making no progress.

Instead, I will ask myself three questions: What ability am I building? What evidence shows that I am improving? And what will I change if the current method stops working?

If I am learning English, I should test whether I can answer an unfamiliar question, not simply count how many times I have read my notes. If I am learning engineering, I should test whether I can explain a real problem and identify what remains unknown.

Hard work matters, but it does not guarantee success. Direction, feedback, opportunity, and circumstances matter too.

**Be patient with results, but impatient with ineffective methods.**

I do not need to become successful overnight. I need today's effort to make tomorrow's decisions clearer and tomorrow's work more capable.

**The goal is not to look busy. The goal is to become capable.**

---

*Note: The presentation, engineering, and desk-fan scenarios illustrate practical distinctions; they are not claims about specific historical events or measured outcomes.*
